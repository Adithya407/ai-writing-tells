# Full catalog: signs of AI writing

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing (WikiProject AI Cleanup advice page), read 7 Nov 2026; page notes run through October 2026. Descriptions are paraphrased. Quoted word lists are the page's "words to watch."

The page calls itself descriptive, not prescriptive. These are observations of what AI text tends to do, not rules for good writing, and any one of them shows up in human writing too. Clusters matter more than single hits.

As of October 2026, the page names the most common signs in newly generated text (besides unambiguous ones such as leaked chat phrases) as:
- Markdown in wikitext (23)
- AI edit summaries (34–38)
- hedging disclaimers about sources (21)
- vague connection or association (5)
- undue emphasis on identification by primary sources (3)

## Content

**1. Undue emphasis on significance, legacy and broader trends.** Tying ordinary facts to a larger movement or lasting importance.
Words to watch: stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a crucial/pivotal/vital/significant/key role/moment, underscores/highlights its importance/significance, reflects broader, symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting, contributing to the, setting the stage for, marking/shaping the, represents/marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted, "distinction [matters/is important] because."
Variants:
- placing the subject amid "debates," or saying it "participated in public discussions"
- a preamble admitting the subject is minor, followed by claims of its importance anyway
- in biology: over-linking a species to its ecosystem, and belaboring conservation status even when it is unknown
- as of mid-2026, often paired with hedging disclaimers (21) that editorialize about why a distinction is important

**2. Canned emphasis on notability, attribution and media coverage.** Listing where a subject was covered, and what kind of outlet each was, instead of saying what the coverage said. The model often attributes its own superficial analysis to those sources. This is more common in tools from 2025 onward.
Words to watch: independent coverage, local/regional/national/[country] media outlets, music/business/tech outlets, trade publications, cited/featured/profiled in, written by a leading expert, active social media presence.
"Maintains an active social media presence" is singled out as rare on Wikipedia before about 2024.

**3. Undue emphasis on identification by primary or tertiary sources.** Stressing that official sites, databases or records "identify" the subject or a fact about it, in stilted, formulaic phrasing. Common from 2026.
Words to watch: databases/records/listings/filings/materials identify/list/describe [subject] as..., was identified by [source] as [fact], is listed in, his/her professional profile lists.

**4. Superficial analyses.** Shallow commentary on significance or impact, usually attached as a present-participle (-ing) clause at the end of a sentence, sometimes with vague attributions. Newer retrieval-enabled bots pin these to named sources whether or not the source says anything similar ("Roger Ebert highlighted the lasting influence").
Words to watch: highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing..., ensuring..., reflecting/symbolizing..., contributing to..., cultivating/fostering..., encompassing..., enhancing..., valuable insights, align/resonate with.

**5. Vague expression of connection or association.** Saying two things are "connected to" or "associated with" each other instead of stating the relationship. "Sources identified John Doe as being associated with leadership of ExampleCorp" stands in for "John Doe was the CEO of ExampleCorp." This is often combined with buzzwords ("particularly/widely associated"), sometimes repeatedly in one passage.
Words to watch: in connection with/to, connected with/to, in association with, associated with.

**6. Vague attributions and overgeneralization of opinions.** Weasel wording, and inflating how many sources hold a view. Common forms:
- presenting one or two sources as a widely held view
- mentioning "reviewers" or "scholars" (plural) while citing one person
- implying a list of examples is partial when the sources give no others

Words to watch: Industry reports, Observers have cited, Experts argue, Some critics argue, several sources/publications (when few are cited), such as (before a list that is complete).

**7. Outline-like conclusions about challenges and future prospects.** A rigid "Challenges" section that:
- opens with "Despite its [positive words], [subject] faces challenges..."
- ends with a vaguely positive assessment, or speculation about initiatives that could help
- often appears alongside a "Future Prospects" section

The sign is the rigid formula, not the mere mention of difficulties.
Words to watch: Despite its... faces several challenges..., Despite these challenges, Challenges and Legacy, Future Outlook.

**8. "Awards and recognition" section.** "X and Y" section headers in general are common. "Awards and recognition," or just "Recognition," is nearly ubiquitous in AI-generated articles, more common than in human articles already tagged as promotional.

## Language and grammar

**9. High density of "AI vocabulary."** One or two of these words can be coincidence. Many of them, many times, in post-2022 text is one of the strongest tells.
Main list: Additionally (especially opening a sentence), align with, boasts (meaning "has"), bolstered, crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate/intricacies, landscape (abstract noun), meticulous/meticulously, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry (abstract noun), testament, underscore (verb), valuable, vibrant.
By era (approximate):
- 2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4): Additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape, meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant.
- Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o): align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant.
- Mid-2025 to mid-2026 (GPT-5): emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing, plus item 2's notability phrasing.
- Mid-2026 on (GPT-6): challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally. The same era shows much more period and comma use, and shorter sentences held around an 8–20 word range.
- Grok specifically: causal, empirical, correlate, and still underscore as of 2026.

Take the list literally: overuse of a word does not imply its synonyms are overused. Mind context, since "underscore" can mean the underline character or incidental music.
"Delve" peaked in 2023 to early 2024 and dropped off sharply in 2025.

**10. Avoidance of basic copulatives ("is"/"are").** Replacing "is" with "serves as," "marks" and similar constructions, and "has" with marketing verbs.
Words to watch: serves as/stands as/marks/functions as/operates as/represents [a], boasts/features/maintains/offers [a], refers to.
Newer output elaborates: "ventured into politics as a candidate" for "was a candidate," "began his career as" for "was."
In opening sentences, "refers to" treats the article as if it were about the term instead of the thing.
This is especially visible in AI copyedits that "improve" plain sentences. Don't confuse it with the present perfect ("has been featured").

**11. Negative parallelisms.** Phrasing as if correcting a misconception the reader never had. Three shapes:
- **Not just X, but also Y:** "Not only... but...," "It is not just..., it's...," including versions spread across two sentences ("He came from a famous family. His life, however, took a path...").
- **Not X, but Y:** "It's not..., it's...," "no..., no..., just...".
- **Y rather than X:** the reversed form, especially common in recent output.

**12. Leads treating lists or broad titles as proper nouns.** The first sentence defines a list or descriptive title as if it were a real-world entity. Examples: "EuroGames editions is the chronological list...," "The 'List of songs about Mexico' is a curated compilation...".

**13. Rule of three.** Heavy use of triplets ("adjective, adjective, adjective"; "short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase"), often to make shallow analysis look thorough. It is a stronger sign where nobody would bother with a flourish, such as an edit summary.

## Style

**14.** Title case in section headings instead of sentence case.
**15.** Too much boldface in prose, sometimes with literal asterisks.
**16.** Inline-header vertical lists, where each bullet opens with a bolded label and a colon.
**17.** Incorrect heading levels: starting with a level-1 heading holding the article title, or using several level-1 headings.
**18.** Emoji at the start of section titles or list items.
**19.** Curly quotation marks and apostrophes (“ ” ’) instead of straight ones.

## Communication intended for the user (pasted in by mistake)

**20. Collaborative communication.** Chatbot correspondence, advice or pre-writing left in the text, including hidden comments with instructions for the editor ("Delete this section before submission," "LEAD: expand once the body exists").
Words to watch: a Wikipedia-style article, I hope this helps, Of course!, Certainly!, You're absolutely right!, Would you like..., is there anything else, let me know, more detailed breakdown, here is a..., [check/verify/confirm]... before [publishing/submitting].

**21. Hedging disclaimers about source availability and recommended usage.** The bot draws attention to gaps instead of filling them. The forms changed by era:
- **2023–24 (didactic):** "it's important to note/remember," safety caveats, disambiguation by jurisdiction.
- **2024–25 (retrieval failures):** claims that information isn't publicly available, often paired with speculation about what it "likely" is, or "maintains a low profile / keeps personal details private." All of it is speculative, including the claim that nothing is documented.
  Words to watch: While specific details are limited/scarce..., not widely available/documented/disclosed, ...in the provided/available sources/search results..., based on available information.
- **Mid-2026 on (corrective framing):** almost unheard of before July 2026. Three patterns:
  - "[claim] should be treated/presented as X rather than Y" (speculative/symbolic vs. verified)
  - "...does not by itself establish..." / the information does not establish a firm conclusion
  - "should not be confused with" (less reliable than the other two)

**22. Phrasal templates and placeholder text.** Fill-in-the-blank text the user forgot to fill:
- bracketed slots: [Your Name], [Article Title 1], [Describe the specific section...]
- "(Add your channel URL here)"
- "<!-- Add if available with citation -->" comments in infoboxes. Check first whether the template ships with such a comment as standard boilerplate.
- date placeholders: 2025-XX-XX in access-date or date fields
- URL placeholders: INSERT_SOURCE_URL_30, PASTE_SPOTIFY_TRACK_URL_HERE, SOURCE_PUBLISHER, and subpages of example.com (example.com/source1, example.com/article, example.com/news)

## Markup irregularities (wiki-specific)

**23. Markdown in wikitext:**
- asterisks for bold
- # for headings
- --- or ---- as dividers
- [text](url) links
- triple-backtick code blocks, often labelled "wikitext" (the least ambiguous form)

**24. Exposed citation internals.** The chatbot's internal citation structure leaks into the text, for example ChatGPT's contentReference[oaicite:1]. This is an unambiguous indicator, and each chatbot has its own pattern.
**25. Other bugs.** Nonexistent categories, and transclusion of nonexistent templates.

## Citations

**26. Broken external links.** Several dead links in a new page, especially ones never captured by the Internet Archive. Exclude links that need institutional access, links mangled by bots, and links truncated by copy-paste.
**27. Invalid DOIs and ISBNs.** A failed ISBN checksum or an unresolvable DOI suggests a hallucinated reference.
**28. DOIs leading to unrelated articles.** Plausible-looking DOIs that resolve to a different paper. The page's example cites a Proceedings of the IEEE paper by an author who had been dead 30+ years. (Note: a 2018–23 VisualEditor bug produced irrelevant low-PMID citations that look similar but usually aren't AI.)
**29. Book citations without page numbers or URLs.** Or with page numbers where the cited page doesn't contain the claim. These are typically general, widely cited books with no link to an online copy.
**30. Incorrect or unconventional reference use.** Broken reference-reuse syntax repeated after every sentence, real but irrelevant references, and ↩ back-arrow characters around footnotes.
**31. utm_source= parameters.** utm_source=openai or utm_source=chatgpt.com (ChatGPT), utm_source=copilot.com (Copilot), referrer=grok.com (Grok). This near-proves the chatbot was used to find sources, but not that it wrote the prose.
**32. Named references declared but unused.** References defined in a references block and never used inline, or named references used without being defined.

## Talk-page comments

**33.** Comments pasted from chatbots, beyond the patterns above, tend to:
- misquote policies and cite invented shortcuts, or just "WP"
- transclude maintenance banners whenever they mention one
- split long comments into titled sections (Markdown, plain text, or level-2/3 headings)
- downplay AI use by claiming human effort, or that the comment reflects their own thoughts
- ask critics to say exactly what needs improving
- dismiss concerns as "unsubstantiated speculation" lacking "concrete" evidence
- urge critics to "focus on improving content" instead of origin
- use a clerk or customer-service voice: "carefully reviewed," apologizing for inconvenience it "may have" caused, "If there are specific issues..., please point them out," "Dear Wikipedia Editorial Team," "I am writing to...," "I hope/trust this message finds you well."

## Edit summaries

The page treats these as rigid formulae: the more a summary deviates, the less likely it is AI. Abbreviations like "ce" argue against AI. Older AI summaries are first-person and show AI vocabulary, emoji, or list/Markdown formatting. Newer ones are exhaustively procedural.

**34. Canned assurance of policy compliance.** Words to watch: ensured that... adheres to, refined, enhanced, enriched, streamlined, improved, in compliance/complies with, Wikipedia [guidelines/style/standards], revised, verifiability, neutrality, neutral tone, encyclopedic tone, clarity, flow. The more assurances stacked in one summary, the stronger the sign. Human editors cite a specific rule briefly ("per MOS:OVERLINK").
**35. Mentions of what was preserved or avoided.** Words to watch: preserved/preserving, retained/retaining, avoided/avoiding, ensured/ensuring, aimed/aiming to. Typical shape: "Revised [section] to improve neutrality while preserving [X]."
**36. Overemphasis on the presence and reliability of citations.** Words to watch: added sourced [information/content/infobox/section], added verified..., added [coverage/citations/references], improved attribution, with [independent/secondary/third-party/peer-reviewed] sources.
**37. Overemphasis on parameter and template names.** Itemized infobox and reference parameter names, often with markup, plus "inline citations" and "internal links."
**38. Reference to AfC review.** "Addressing reviewer feedback by...", a statement so obvious a human rarely writes it.

## Miscellaneous

**39. Pronounced shift in writing style.** Suddenly flawless grammar compared with the editor's other writing, especially writing from before Nov 2022. A long-time AI user's style also tracks each year's models. The reverse holds too: a style consistent with pre-2022 edits argues against AI.
**40. "Submission statements" in AfC drafts.** Notes addressed to the reviewer arguing the draft is neutral, well sourced and notable, or describing how AI was used.
**41. Pre-placed maintenance templates.** For example {{AfC submission|d}}, which pre-declines the draft with no reasoning, and implausible maintenance or protection tags on a brand-new page.
**42. Canned user pages.** A rigid format: Welcome To My User Page!, About Me, My Interests, Let's Connect!, with emoji, inline-header lists and bold (or Markdown attempts at bold).
**43. Permissions gaming.** Rapid benign-looking AI rewrites across unrelated articles to raise an account's edit count. Use this only in one direction: suspected gaming suggests AI, but rapid AI edits are not proof of gaming.
**44. Differences between LLMs.** Each model has its own idiolect. ChatGPT and Grok lean on broader context. Gemini and Claude are more concise. ChatGPT is historically the most used chatbot for Wikipedia edits.
**45. Biases in content.** Pro-authoritarian bias on some political prompts, even in American frontier models (2026). Responses in Chinese are more so. Some models are more willing to criticize governments in freer countries than repressive ones.

## Historical indicators (older models; rarer now)

**46. Promotional, advertisement-like language (2023–2025).** Travel-guide or press-release tone, but usually avoiding outright superlatives like "the best." It faded from about 2024 unless the user asks for it.
Words to watch: boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, featuring, diverse array.
**47. Statements about knowledge cutoffs (2022–2023).** Words to watch: Up to my last training update, as of my last knowledge update.
**48. Section summaries.** "Conclusion" sections, or paragraphs that end by restating their own point. Words to watch: In summary, In conclusion, Overall.
**49. Prompt refusal.** Words to watch: as an AI language model, as a large language model, I cannot offer medical advice, but I can..., I'm sorry. Now rare.
**50. Overuse of em dashes (2022 to September 2026).** Em dashes used where people would use commas, parentheses or colons, often in a punchy sales-like way, and usually with spaces around them (contrary to standard typography). This is more common in discussions than in articles and is weak on its own. A July 2026 study found Claude was the only current model using em dashes more than professional writers; ChatGPT used fewer. Since September 2026 the newest models are increasingly hard-coded to avoid em dashes entirely. Their punctuation variety drops as a result, and over-reliance on commas and periods has become its own sign.
**51. Abrupt cut-offs.** Text that stops mid-sentence (older ChatGPT token limits). This can also come from a bad copy-paste or a copyright violation.
**52. Outdated access-date parameters.** Access-dates noticeably older than the edit, for example a Dec 2025 article citing access-date=12 December 2024 throughout. Rare in newer chatbots, and legitimate in copied citations.
**53. Lexical diversity / elegant variation.** Cycling synonyms to avoid repeating a word, from a repetition penalty in older models (for example "Soviet artistic constraints," then "the confines of state-imposed artistic norms," then "the constraints imposed by the Soviet regime"). Non-native writers taught to avoid repetition do this too, as Italian schools do.

## Signs of human writing

- **Age.** Text added before 30 Nov 2022 (ChatGPT's public launch) can be ruled out. Who Wrote That? and WikiBlame find when text was inserted.
- **Explaining choices.** A human can usually explain an odd edit, such as a typo in a URL, and produce the real source passage.
- **Syntax.** These are observed to be more common in human-written Wikipedia text than in AI text:
  - simple is/has phrases ("there is a," "it has a")
  - plain words where AI picks stiff or euphemistic synonyms: wrote/authored, moved/relocated, used/utilized, tried/attempted, died/passed away
  - superlative or definitive statements: "one of the best," "is the only," "was the first"
  - qualifiers and intensifiers: very, perhaps, tends to
  - isolated wordy constructions: as a result of, in order to, all of the, a part of, the fact that

## Ineffective indicators (don't rely on them)

False accusations drive away new editors. Style-only detection is harder than it looks, and these are weak or misleading:
- perfect grammar
- a mix of casual and formal registers, or "clinical" plus "emotional" language
- "bland" or "robotic" prose
- "fancy," academic or formal prose in general (only specific words correlate)
- transition words on their own (only a few, like Additionally, are overused)
- unsourced content (most such articles predate LLMs, and modern bots do cite, though not always accurately)
- bizarre wikitext (usually extension bugs or VisualEditor slips)
- correct wikitext

## Caveats on detection

- AI detectors (GPTZero, Pangram) beat chance but have non-trivial error rates. A high detector score is not grounds for speedy deletion.
- In 2025 studies:
  - general human detection was near chance
  - one study of German theses found 57% recognition for AI texts and 64% for human texts
  - "expert" annotators who use LLMs heavily caught 90% of AI text, with 3.3% false positives
- Human writing is drifting toward LLM style, but LLM output is not becoming more like human writing.
